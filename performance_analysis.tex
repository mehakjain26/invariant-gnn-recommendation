\begin{abstract}
Traditional recommender system research evaluates models using random splits, creating an illusion of high performance driven by popularity bias. However, when evaluated chronologically, these models catastrophically fail on drifting subpopulations (items falling out of favor). To rescue performance, we apply Causal Inference metrics---Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) and Variance Risk Extrapolation (V-REx)---to strip away popularity shortcuts and learn true, invariant semantic affinities. 

Our empirical testing acts as an A/B test between two highly distinct graph architectures. On the hyper-sparse, dynamically chaotic Amazon dataset (0.06\% density), IRM completely collapses, while V-REx remains resilient and boosts subgroup robustness by 19\%. Conversely, on the moderately dense, structured Yelp2018 graph (0.13\% density), the increased signal resolution provides clean enough batch gradients for IRM to function, yielding a massive 71\% robustness boost. 

Ultimately, this structural autopsy reveals that while IRM offers precise feature alignment in dense environments, it is fundamentally fragile against batch noise. We establish V-REx as the mandatory, stable regularization baseline for securing performance against distribution shifts in ultra-sparse, commercial engagement graphs.
\end{abstract}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{The Goldilocks Zone (Yelp, $\rho = 0.51$):} Trends shift significantly (exposing standard baselines), but do so in a structured, systematic way. This provides the perfect gradient stability for V-REx and IRM to successfully separate intrinsic features from transient popularity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Comparison 1: V-REx vs. IRM Performance}
When observing Tables \ref{tab:amazon_ablation} and \ref{tab:yelp_ablation}, a stark divergence emerges in how the two invariant penalties respond to the structure of the dataset.

\subsection{Why IRM Fails on Amazon}
On the hyper-sparse Amazon dataset, IRM entirely fails to improve subgroup robustness, with Falling Recall strictly degrading (0.0124) compared to the baseline. This failure is rooted in the mathematical collision between IRM's \textit{Feature Alignment} mechanism and Amazon's \textit{Chaotic Drift} ($\rho = 0.24$). 

IRM functions by locating the intersection of gradients across environments. Yelp exhibits \textit{Structured Drift}, with an \textbf{Item Extinction Rate} of \textbf{5.2\%} and a \textbf{Top-100 Overlap} of \textbf{48\%}, allowing IRM to locate a stable causal bridge. Amazon exhibits \textbf{Chaotic Drift}, where the Item Extinction Rate nearly doubles to \textbf{9.8\%}, and the Top-100 Overlap plummets to only \textbf{23\%}. This proving that even the most globally popular items undergo violent transition. This results in a ``Causal Intersection'' which is effectively a null space. When high penalties ($\lambda=100$) force the model to satisfy these impossible gradient constraints against extreme batch noise (0.06\% density), the network undergoes catastrophic accuracy collapse.

\subsection{Why V-REx Survives}
Conversely, V-REx successfully decouples transit popularity on Amazon, yielding a +19\% boost. V-REx relies on \textbf{Loss Alignment}. It simply monitors the final scalar loss values, asking: \textit{``Is the variance of the error the same across environments?''} Because loss variance is a macroscopic, zeroth-order statistic aggregated over the entire batch, it acts as a ``blunt but stable'' regularizer. It proves incredibly resilient to microscopic batch noise, allowing it to bootstrap causal embeddings where delicate gradients shatter.

\subsection{The Success of Both on Yelp}
On Yelp, the dynamics shift completely. The moderately increased density (0.13\%) provides clean batch gradients. This naturally enables IRM's precise gradient-matching constraints to successfully extract invariants obscured by noise in Amazon, allowing both V-REx and IRM to yield $\sim$70\% boosts.

\section{Comparison 2: The +19\% vs. +70\% Improvement Gap}
While V-REx survives the sparsity of Amazon, it hits a hard ceiling: a $+19\%$ improvement on Falling items. In stark contrast, both methods achieve massive $>70\%$ improvements on Yelp. This massive performance gap is dictated by three underlying structural forces within the data.

\subsection{Signal-per-Item Resolution}
The total density of the graph is fundamentally limited by the number of active items. Even though Amazon possesses nearly double the total interactions of Yelp (~2.9M vs ~1.5M), it must sustain 2.4x as many items (91,599 vs 38,048). 

Using the totals from Table \ref{tab:dataset_stats}, the average \textit{Signal-per-Item} (Total Interactions / Items) drops significantly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MovieLens-1M:} $\sim$306.7 users per item (Ultra-High Resolution).
    \item \textbf{Yelp2018:} $\sim$39.4 users per item (Moderate Resolution).
    \item \textbf{Amazon-Books:} $\sim$31.6 users per item (Low Resolution).
\end{itemize}
In LightGCN architectures, node embeddings are formulated by aggregating topological neighborhoods. Yelp provides ``thicker'' neighborhoods, offering a higher-resolution snapshot of item semantics. Amazon spreads its signal too thinly across 91,599 nodes, causing causal vectors to blur into the noise floor, which sets a forced ceiling on how much an invariant model can improve relative to the baseline.

\subsection{Degree Distribution and The Smooth Tail}
The topology of the long tail heavily influences environmental separability. Yelp exhibits a \textit{Smooth Tail} (Figure \ref{fig:degree_dist}). A higher percentage of its items populate the middle tiers (100–1,000 interactions), creating a gradient of popularity that allows models to learn real causal features. Amazon exhibits a \textit{Fragmented Tail}. A vast majority of its 91,000 items sit exactly at a low baseline floor, making them mathematically indistinguishable to environmental groupings.

\section{Ablation of Loss Aggregation: Vanilla vs. Balanced BPR}
In addition to the invariant penalty, we investigate the impact of the base BPR loss aggregation method. In the standard architecture (\textit{Vanilla}), BPR loss is calculated as a single mean over all triplets in a batch. In the \textit{Balanced} variant, we calculate the mean BPR loss per environment (Rising, Falling, Stable) independently and sum these means, effectively forcing the model to weight all environments equally regardless of their size.

As shown in Table \ref{tab:bpr_ablation}, \textbf{Environment Balancing in the base BPR loss is generally counter-productive for Falling Recall but beneficial for Rising Recall.} On the Yelp dataset, switching to Balanced BPR suppresses the Falling Recall gains from 0.0295 down to 0.0184. However, on Amazon, Balanced BPR doubles the Rising Recall (0.0005 to 0.0012). We identify three theoretical reasons for this divergence.

\paragraph{The Sparse Rising Signal}
Rising items represent the "coldest" part of the tail during training, often possessing only 1-5 interactions before their engagement explodes in the test set. Vanilla BPR effectively ignores these items due to their negligible contribution to the mean batch loss. Balanced BPR, by up-weighting the "Rising" environment, forces the model to attend to these nascent signals. This provides a minor boost to Rising Recall (as seen in Amazon), though often at the cost of the stable collaborative backbone.

\paragraph{The ``Backbone'' Principle}
The ``Stable'' environment (~80\% of the item population) provides the high-quality collaborative backbone of the graph. It represents the reliable, long-term latent preferences of users (e.g., genre or cuisine affinity). Vanilla BPR allows these stable clicks to correctly dominate the gradient, building a high-fidelity foundation for the node embeddings.

\paragraph{Weighting as Noise Multiplier}
In Balanced BPR, because the ``Falling'' and ``Rising'' environments are much smaller, a single click in those environments is effectively weighted $\sim$10x higher than a stable click. Since these drifting items are inherently more chaotic (experiencing violent shifts in engagement), over-weighting them introduces significant ``jitter'' into the embeddings. The model fixates on the shifting tails before it has mastered the stable core.

\paragraph{Foundation vs. Regularization}
We conclude that BPR must act as the \textit{Foundation} (building the map), while IRM and V-REx act as the \textit{Regularization} (correcting the bias). Attempting to ``balance'' the foundation via Balanced BPR results in a blurry latent space. True invariant extrapolation requires a robust, high-resolution latent map (learned via Vanilla BPR) upon which the invariant penalties can then precisely operate to decouple spurious from causal signals.

As shown in Tables \ref{tab:env_amazon} and \ref{tab:env_yelp}, the number of environments ($E$) acts as a critical "Regularization Multiplier," where higher values do not necessarily translate to better robustness.

\paragraph{Constraint Inflation (IRM Sensitivity)}
IRM functions by enforcing gradient consistency across all $E$ environments. With $E=2$, the model only needs to satisfy a single "bridge" of gradient matching. This is a relatively loose constraint that allows the model to retain accuracy while gaining robustness. However, with $E=5$, the model must satisfy \textbf{four simultaneous gradient constraints}. In sparse collaborative graphs, it is mathematically difficult to find a single parameter space that satisfies five distinct environmental gradients. This leads to the optimization collapse observed in Table \ref{tab:env_yelp}, where Falling Recall on Yelp plummets by 50\% when moving from $E=2$ to $E=5$.

\paragraph{The "Rising" Subgroup Fragmentation}
Across all datasets, Rising Recall consistently degrades as $E$ increases. Rising items are already extremely sparse, often possessing only 1--5 interactions during training. When splitting the training set into 5 environments, the signal for these items in each environment becomes so diluted that the invariant penalty (IRM or V-REx) treats them as pure noise. While $E=2$ or $E=3$ provides large enough environments to preserve a "detectable" rising signal, $E=5$ fragments it beyond repair.

\paragraph{V-REx Resilience vs. IRM Fragility}
V-REx is notably more stable as $E$ increases compared to IRM. Because V-REx utilizes a loss-variance penalty (a macroscopic, zeroth-order scalar) rather than per-parameter gradient matching, it is less sensitive to the "micro-noise" introduced by finer environmental partitioning. As seen in the Yelp results ($\lambda=1.0$), $E=5$ actually outperforms $E=2$ for V-REx, as the model benefits from a higher-resolution sampling of out-of-distribution variance. However, even V-REx shows diminishing returns beyond $E=3$ as the collaborative signal begins to blur.

\paragraph{The "Sweet Spot" ($E=3$)}
The standard 3-environment split (Rising, Falling, Stable) consistently emerges as the optimal "Goldilocks" zone. it provides enough granularity to distinguish between subpopulations while maintaining large enough batches for the invariant gradients to remain stable and avoid catastrophic accuracy trade-offs.

\section{The Asymmetry of Discovery: Why Rising Recall Lags}
Across all experiments and datasets, a massive performance asymmetry exists: \textbf{Falling Recall is consistently 20x--30x higher than Rising Recall} (e.g., 0.0148 vs. 0.0005 on Amazon baseline). We identify this as the fundamental gap between \textit{Knowledge Retention} and \textit{Future Discovery}.

\subsection{Falling Recall: Leveraging Known Semantics}
Falling items were popular during the training period. The model has already learned high-fidelity, stable embeddings for these items through thousands of user interactions. Even though these items are losing overall engagement in the test set, their semantic identity remains robust in the latent space. If a user's long-term preferences still align with a "dying" niche, the model can accurately retrieve the item because it already knows exactly where it sits in the embedding manifold.

\subsection{Rising Recall: The Zero-Shot Discovery Problem}
In contrast, Rising items represent the "void" of the training set. These items were niche or nascent in the past, often possessing only 1--5 training interactions. Consequently, the model has failed to learn a meaningful representation for them; they are effectively "random noise" in the latent space. When these items suddenly explode in popularity during the test window, the model is being asked to perform \textbf{Zero-Shot Discovery}. Without content-based metadata, a collaborative filter has no mechanism to "predict" the success of an item it has never seen perform well. This creates a hard mathematical floor for Rising Recall that architectural depth and even invariant penalties struggle to overcome.

\section{Discussion: The Fundamental Boundary for Causal Recommendation}
Our findings establish a clear boundary for when theoretically superior causal models like IRM fail in commercial settings. Beyond global density, we isolate three deeper structural forces that cause algorithmic collapse.

\subsection{User-to-Item Ratio (The Collaborative Bridge)}
The datasets possess contrasting User-to-Item ratios: Yelp ($\sim$0.81) vs. Amazon ($\sim$0.57). In Amazon, having nearly two items for every one user creates a ``User-Sparse'' graph. Since IRM incorporates first-order gradient matching, it is highly sensitive to direct 1-hop collaborative connections. The diluted connectivity in Amazon prevents the 1st-order gradients from effectively capturing the underlying causal links, rendering IRM's feature alignment mechanism ineffective.

\subsection{Space Resolution and Spurious Noise}
The Amazon search space ($52k \times 91k$) is approximately 4x larger than that of Yelp. In a massive, sparse matrix, the probability that a random interaction appears as a ``Spurious Signal'' increases exponentially. IRM is notoriously sensitive to outliers because its penalty squares environmental gradients. In the hyper-sparse void of the Amazon graph, noisy gradient ``spikes'' likely dominated the penalty calculation, preventing the model from converging on truly invariant features.

\section{Conclusion}
Our study proves that evaluating commercial recommenders natively on temporal splits exposes critical popularity bias flaws. Exploring these dynamics through an Amazon / Yelp comparison reveals a systemic boundary for Causal modeling. While IRM offers precise feature alignment optimal for structured (Yelp) platforms, V-REx provides the mandatory stability required for successful invariant recommendations within hyper-sparse, noisy engagement graphs (Amazon). 

Furthermore, we isolate a fundamental \textbf{"Asymmetry of Discovery"}: models are significantly better at leveraging historical knowledge to track "Falling" items than they are at predicting the emergence of "Rising" items. We find that architectural depth, loss aggregation integrity, and the structural resolvability of the item-user bridge are mandatory prerequisites for successful causal extrapolation in long-tail recommendation ecosystems.
